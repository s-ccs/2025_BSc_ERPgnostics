# Thesis Structure

## General Advice

- **Think before you write.** Have an outline. Know what each section will
  contain and how to approach it. Brain dumps stay brain dumps.
- Don't stick slavishly to a recipe structure. Merge, split, reorder sections
  as needed.
- The thesis must be **honest** (state all limitations clearly) and
  **self-contained** (don't write just for locals).
- A thesis presents a **hypothesis** and its confirmation. It is NOT an
  activity report.
- **Write the intro first** (to define the roadmap), then **rewrite it last**
  (to ensure consistency with what you actually did).

## Section-by-Section Guidance

### Abstract
- 1-3 paragraphs. State: problem, assumptions, solution idea, results.
- Avoid non-standard terms and acronyms.
- Must be readable completely on its own, detached from the thesis.
- No references in the abstract.

### Introduction
- Introduce the problem gently. Give the reader an appreciation of the
  difficulty.
- Clearly state: vision/aims, what problem you solve, why it is important,
  and (at a high level) your approach.
- The reader should know after the intro what to expect in the rest.
- Don't bore the reader. Don't make outrageous claims.
- Don't create expectations you can't deliver on ("bait and switch").
- Pick up every thread spun in the intro later in the thesis.
- Provide enough context about limitations and assumptions.

### Background / Exposition of Problem
- Go into detail about the problem.
- Give a thorough, complete discussion — enough so an educated reader outside
  your specialty can appreciate it.
- Don't call it "Exposition of the Problem" — find a better title.

### Related Work (Literature Review)
- **Really important.** If you can't demonstrate knowledge of the field, you
  demonstrate cluelessness.
- Explain what has been done, what's different, and (if you're good) why your
  thing hasn't been done before.
- There is always related work. Vagueness = obvious you haven't done homework.
- Report old work in contemporary language, not in the original paper's
  terminology.
- Be fair: state prior work's achievements neutrally.
  - BAD: "Doe investigates X but fails to account for Y"
  - GOOD: "Doe investigates X under the implied assumption of constant Y"
- Normal assumption: prior work is good, you're taking it further.

### Design of Your Solution (Methods)
- Give a **general overview first**, then go into detail (top-down).
- Discuss design tradeoffs BEFORE presenting your chosen design.
  Don't use: "I did it this way. I could have done it that way, but..."
- Show that you thought things through and arrived at the best solution.
- Be forthright about limitations and assumptions. Justify shortcuts.

### Implementation
- Separate design from implementation.
- Give enough detail that someone can reproduce your work from the thesis
  alone, without looking at source code.
- Clearly state implementation limitations and justify them.

### Experiments / Results
- Think about what makes sense to measure. What do you want to learn?
- Think about success criteria and how to establish you've met them.
- Explain surprising results. "Surprise, surprise" without explanation =
  you're clueless or made a mistake.
- Provide standard deviations alongside averages. Always.
- Give enough detail on what and HOW you measure so someone can reproduce.

### Discussion
- Discuss and explain results. Show how they support your thesis.
- **Separate objective facts clearly from subjective discussion.**
- If the reader doesn't understand your results, you probably don't either.

### Conclusions
- Don't just discuss — draw real conclusions. What can we LEARN?
- Separate discussion/interpretation from conclusions clearly.
  "Conclusion creep" = surrendering scientific objectivity.
- Identify ALL shortcomings/limitations and discuss how they could be fixed
  ("future work").
