# Style and Grammar Rules

## Active Voice (CRITICAL)

Overuse of passive voice is the MOST annoying mistake in undergraduate theses.

- If >= 25% of sentences use passive voice, the prose is poor.
- Passive voice often masks who did the work. Show ownership!
- BAD: "A suitable protocol was designed to cope with that situation."
- GOOD: "We designed a protocol to cope with that situation."

**Heiser will mark students down for excessive passive voice, no matter how
good the content is.**

## Use of "We" — Inclusive, Not Royal

Scientific writing uses first person plural ("we"). This INCLUDES theses.

The "we" is **inclusive** (= "you, the reader, and I together"), NOT the
royal "we" (= "I, the king").

### Rules:
- Use present and future tense with "we" — this takes the reader along.
- Generally do NOT use past tense with "we" — that sounds like royal we.

### Examples:
- GOOD: "We will now look at the dependency of power on load."
- GOOD: "We run the system at maximum performance and measure temperature.
  We obtain the results shown in Figure 5."
- BAD: "We obtained the results shown in Figure 5." (royal we — the reader
  wasn't there when you did this)

## Tense

- **Present tense** for general truths, established facts, describing what the
  thesis does: "Section 3 presents our approach."
- **Present tense** for taking the reader through the work: "We observe that..."
- **Past tense** only for specific historical events: "Smith et al. (2020)
  showed that..."

## Buzzwords — AVOID

Words like "novel", "new", "innovative", "groundbreaking" are useless fillers.
If it weren't novel, you wouldn't be writing about it. These words create the
impression that doing something new is unusual for you.

Also avoid popular trade-press terminology — use standard scientific vocabulary.

## Hyphens, En-Dashes, Em-Dashes

### Hyphen (-)
Used for compound words and ad-hoc compound adjectives:
- "single-address-space operating system" (without hyphens, "space" would
  qualify "operating system")
- No hyphen if first part is an inflected adverb: "badly overclocked CPU"

### En-dash (--)
Used for ranges: "RAM sizes of 0.5--64 GiB" (no spaces around it for
single items, spaces for multi-word items: "12 March -- 15 May")

### Em-dash (---)
Used as separator, similar to semicolon. Use sparingly — excessive
parenthetical remarks indicate lack of clarity.

## Commas

Key rules:
1. **Single comma** after a longish introductory element:
   "Before the close of the last Ice Age, Tasmania was joined to..."
2. **Pairs of commas** to set off parenthetical or appositional phrases.
3. **Serial comma**: not required in British/Australian English, but add it
   where needed for clarity.
4. General rule: add commas where they help understanding, leave them where
   they don't.

## Colons and Lists

- Colons introduce examples or details. The next word is NOT capitalised
  (unless it would be anyway).
- Vertical lists (bullet/numbered) are introduced by a colon.
- List item formatting depends on complexity:
  - Short items (few words): no capitalisation, no punctuation
  - Items with internal punctuation but not full sentences: no capitalisation,
    semicolons, period at end
  - Full-sentence items: capitalised, each ends with period

## Quotation Marks

- Used for **quotations** and **irony only**. NOT for introducing new terms.
- New terms are introduced with *italics*.
- Use double quotation marks. Single only for quotes within quotes.

## Contractions

Don't use contractions (don't, can't, it's) in formal prose — they sound
colloquial. (Yes, this very document violates this rule for readability.)

## Split Infinitives

Acceptable if it improves readability. Use sparingly.

## "Like" vs "Such as"

- **Such as**: example IS a member of the set.
  "Languages such as Python and Julia..."
- **Like**: example RESEMBLES members of the set but is not one.
  "It behaves like a traditional classifier..."
