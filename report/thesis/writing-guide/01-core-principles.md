# Core Principles

## The Two Cs

### 1. Be Clear
Clarity applies at every level:

- **Word level**: each word is precise, no ambiguity.
- **Sentence level**: each sentence has exactly one unambiguous meaning.
- **Paragraph level**: each paragraph has one clear purpose, talks about one
  thing only. Keep paragraphs to 2-4 sentences. Break at natural transition
  points, not mid-argument.
- **Section level**: each section has a clear, coherent purpose and message.
  Avoid sections longer than about one page; break up or sub-structure if needed.
- **Whole thesis**: the thesis has ONE clear, consistent message. You should be
  able to state it in one short sentence. Then stick to it everywhere.

### 2. Be Concise
- If something can be said in a sentence instead of a paragraph, say it in a
  sentence.
- Conciseness must not come at the expense of clarity.
- "Gainful compression": editing typically shortens text by 20-25% while
  improving clarity.
- **Jay's Rule of Thumb**: cover up parts of the text with your thumb. If it
  doesn't change the meaning, cut it.

## Maintaining User State

Think of the reader's mind as memory that you are managing:

1. **Reasonable initial state**: For a BSc thesis, assume a peer who completed
   the relevant courses but knows nothing about your specific topic area.

2. **Self-contained**: Don't assume the reader has read any specific prior work.
   Cite it and summarize the relevant parts.

3. **Define-before-use**: Never use a term before you have explained it. If you
   must defer an explanation, provide a forward reference AND a brief informal
   description at the point of first use.

4. **Strategic redundancy**: For each major section:
   - (1) Say what you're going to say (1-3 sentence overview)
   - (2) Say it (the actual content)
   - (3) Say what you just said (1-3 sentence summary of take-aways)
   Use this for main chapters AND top-level sections within chapters.

5. **Refresh stale state**: If a term was explained many pages ago and then not
   used, gently remind the reader. Example:
   > "In Section 2 we defined *gizmo* to be something every cool kid wants.
   > Now we will..."

6. **Consistent terminology**: Never use two terms interchangeably unless you
   explicitly state they are synonyms — and then pick ONE and stick to it.

7. **Circular dependencies**: If A requires B and B requires A, first give a
   brief informal explanation of both, then follow up with rigorous definitions.

## Readability

- Reading the thesis should be an enjoyable experience, not hard work.
- Write in a lucid, engaging style.
- Explain things **top-down**, not bottom-up.
- Sloppy presentation signals sloppy content.
- The thesis is NOT an experience report. **Focus on the destination, not the
  journey.** Detours and blind alleys are mostly irrelevant unless they teach
  general truths about design tradeoffs.
